Return on investment

Saved per dollar spent, as you treat further down the list. In the real world this often humps rather than simply falling — the highest-risk can be harder to turn around (though they may carry a larger liability), so the very top isn’t always the best value.

Total benefit

Liability averted minus all treatment cost. The peak is the optimal investment point — treat past it and each extra dollar returns less than $1. Fiscal only (see note).

Fiscal lens only. “Net fiscal benefit” counts only financial savings (reduced forward liability). It deliberately sets aside the wider wellbeing benefits a successful intervention creates — better employment, education and health, and reduced suicide and crime — which are real and often far larger, and call for a multi-dimensional approach discussed elsewhere. Financial indicators are used here purely to illustrate the targeting and return-on-investment concepts.

About the model. For understanding the concepts — not for planning. It is a deliberately simplified version of the analysis an economist or analyst would do, useful because it draws out the core ideas and trade-offs. Under the hood it uses an equal-variance binormal ROC model: the AUC dial sets a single smooth risk-score curve, and the policy slider picks an operating point on it. Because of this, the AUC here reads slightly higher (~0.1) than an empirical AUC measured across real discrete decision thresholds (as in Mansell et al., Reframing child protection, 2011). Treat the numbers as illustrative of the dynamics, not as operational forecasts.
